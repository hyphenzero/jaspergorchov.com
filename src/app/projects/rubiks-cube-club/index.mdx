Building a website for a school club is a different kind of challenge than building for a client. The audience knows each other, the content changes every week, and the whole thing needs to be maintainable by students who might not have touched HTML before.

The Rubik's Cube Club at Lake Oswego High School needed exactly that — a home on the web where members could find meeting times, track their personal bests, and point new recruits who wanted to learn.

## What the site does

The site serves three main roles. First, it's an information hub: meeting times, room locations, and a calendar of upcoming competitions are always a click away. Second, it's a showcase: member profiles with their personal records, favorite puzzles, and a bit about how they got started. Third, it's a resource library: beginner tutorials, algorithm sheets, and links to the timers and tools the club uses during practice.

The leaderboard is the centerpiece. It pulls from a shared spreadsheet that the club officers update after each meeting, displaying best times by event — 2x2, 3x3, 4x4, and the ever-controversial pyraminx. The table sorts by event and highlights the current school record holders.

## Design and build

I kept the design playful but not childish — a saturated color palette that nods to the six faces of a Rubik's Cube, with plenty of white space so the content breathes. The layout is responsive by necessity: most of the club's traffic comes from students checking meeting times on their phones between classes.

The site is built as a static Next.js site deployed on Vercel, with the spreadsheet data fetched at build time via a serverless function. That means the leaderboard is always up to date without needing a database or a CMS, and the site loads instantly because it's all pre-rendered HTML.

## What I learned

This project taught me how to design for a very specific kind of maintainability. Every decision — from the spreadsheet-driven data layer to the deliberately simple component structure — was made with the next person in mind. The goal wasn't to build something impressive; it was to build something that would still be running smoothly long after I graduated.
